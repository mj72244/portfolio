# Rapid Instructional Design

Rapid Instructional Design (RID) is a flexible and accelerated method for developing training and educational content, emphasizing efficiency to deliver high-quality learning experiences in less time than traditional models like ADDIE.

Its emphasis on streamlining the development phase and expediting online training process is appealing when creating targeted training with limited resources and in a time constrained environment.

Unlike models such as ADDIE, Rapid Instructional Design does not have a fixed or  rigid set of phases. In many ways RID resembles the software development model of Agile, which uses “sprints” to rapidly iterate during software development. In a similar fashion RID emphasizes iterative cycles for course development.

One example of stages of RID from University of Maryland Global Campus (n.d.) are:

* Analysis and Needs Assessment: As with other models, development begins with analysis of learning needs and goals. Developers work with subject matter experts, stakeholders, and learners to identify objectives and leverage existing resources.
* Design and Planning: Create a high level plan, determine content and format, and prioritize meaningful interaction.
* Content Development: Repurpose existing content when possible, otherwise create engaging content in collaboration with stakeholders and subject matter experts.
* Prototype and Testing: Create a small scale version of the instructional material to gather feedback from learners and stakeholders.
* Deployment and Iteration: Deploy the course and gather feedback from learners through surveys, test analysis, and other mechanisms to improve the course.
* Evaluation and Maintenance: Assess the effectiveness of the material. In the case of EMS continuing education this includes quality assurance review of cases, feedback from individual clinicians, and input from EMS supervisors.

  

## Application to EMS Continuing Education Challenges

A key challenge for EMS continuing education is how RID can be leveraged to create content for several hundred clinicians who complete the course over just a few weeks, a timeframe that does not realistically allow much iteration and improvement during delivery while still meeting regulatory requirements for minimum contact hours of education and ensuring all clinicians receive a uniform understanding of current protocols and practice.

Strategies to address this during development include:

* Creating an initial prototype (e.g.,a slide deck and set of case scenarios).
* Sharing it with a small group: subject matter experts, a few representative learners (including a mix of new and veteran paramedics), and stakeholders.
* Gathering feedback (e.g., “The sickle cell crisis scenario feels too basic” or “Add an annotated version of the DNR protocol that addresses how to engage medical control”).
* Refining in short cycles (hours or days).

This front-loaded iteration ensures the delivered version is improved by miniature prototypes and feedback loops, even if the course is a one-time offering.

An additional challenge is soliciting timely feedback from busy medical directors and EMS clinicians.

For in-person courses, real-time adjustments can be made during delivery by incorporating polls, tools such as Kahoot!, and breakout discussions.
