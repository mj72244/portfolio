# Dick and Carey Model

The Dick and Carey Model is an instructional framework developed by Walter Dick and Lou Carey. The model treats instructional design as a system with interconnecting components. It emphasizes front-end analysis, precise objectives, and iterative evaluation.

It consists of ten main steps (University of Maryland Global Campus, n.d.):

1.   Identify Instructional Goals: Analyze performance gaps that instruction can solve, then define broad, measurable goals (e.g., “Paramedics will apply safe pain management protocols in special case scenarios”).

2.   Conduct Instructional Analysis: Break down goals into specific steps, skills, and knowledge required to achieve them.

3.   Identify Entry Behaviors (analyze learners): Examine the learners’ prior knowledge, experience, motivation, and demographics.  

4.   Write Performance Objectives: Create specific and measurable learning conditions, performance, and criteria (e.g., "Given a case simulation of renal colic, the paramedic will select and administer appropriate analgesia within protocol limits with 100% accuracy").

5.   Develop Assessment Instruments: Design performance assessments aligned with the objectives (e.g., scenario based quizzes and simulation scoring rubrics)

6.   Develop Instructional Strategy: Plan how content will be delivered, including pre-instructional activities, presentation of content, and practice and feedback.

7.   Develop and Select Instructional Materials: Create or choose resources like videos, interactive scenarios, or other content.

8.   Design and Conduct Formative Evaluation: Test the instructions with a small group to gather information on effectiveness.

9.   Revise Instruction: Use formative evaluation to make improvements to content, activities, and assessments.

10.        Conduct Summative Evaluation:  Evaluate the instruction’s effectiveness in meeting real world goals (e.g., quality assurance review of actual EMS incidents).

## Implications for Instructional Design

The model provides a systematic approach to instructional design helping align objectives, instruction, and evaluation. It emphasizes learner centered analysis early in the process and iterative improvement. The models focus on performance objectives and assessment supports mastery across diverse learners. Compared to ADDIE its more detailed steps help support the instructional designer creating educational courses.

## Evaluation of Strengths and Limitations

The minicourse “Pain Management for EMS: Special Case Scenarios” is a concise continuing education course targeting paramedics with diverse experience levels and organizational roles, focusing on ethical and protocol base decision making in uncommon scenarios. 

### Strengths

·      The model’s front-loaded analysis supports the design of the course. Step 1 (identifying goals) and Step 2 (instructional analysis) address the identified knowledge gap in special case pain management. This analysis leverages previous conversations in continuing education sessions and quality assurance reviews of past incidents.

·      Step 3 (analyzing learners) directly supports UDL considerations by accounting for the diversity of experience and career stage.

·      Step 4 (write performance objectives) and Step 5 (assessments) pairs well with needs for demonstrated mastery and training time requirements.

·      Iterative evaluation of training helps refine current and future training, so they remain relevant to busy EMS professionals.

·      Overall, the more detailed framework may help newer instructional designers compared to a model such as ADDIE.

### Limitations

·      For a brief 45-minute course the full 10-step process can be heavy weight and time intensive to apply fully. This is especially the case when considering that the rate of advancement in medicine and protocols limits the applicability of many continuing education courses to a small handful of years (2-3).
