# Overview of the Successive Approximation Model (SAM)

Developed by Michael Allen, SAM is an iterative approach to creating learning experiences that leverages rapid prototyping, collaboration, and refining on the fly. Instead of of aiming for perfection on the fly, it builds successive approximations of the course, including feedback early and often. While it’s associated with eLearning, Michael Allen stresses that it is appropriate to the instructional design of any course (Allen & Sites, 2012).

In his book Leaving ADDIE for SAM: An Agile Model for Developing  the Best Learning Experiences (2012), Michael Allen draws a parallel between instructional design and software development. He paints ADDIE as the classic waterfall process: rigid, sequential, and prone to costly do-overs if something goes wrong. Just like in software’s waterfall model where water does not flow upstream, ADDIE locks you into a linear path. SAM, by contrast borrows from agile software methodologies, emphasizing quick iteration, stakeholder involvement, and flexibility to pivot as needed.  

## Overview of SAM1 and SAM2

SAM comes in two flavors to match the scope of the project:

SAM1: A lightweight version. A Three iteration cycle with evaluation, then looping through design and development. For small teams iterating through a prototype, test, and refine loop.

SAM2: For larger projects. It expands SAM1 to eight steps across the three core phases adding more structure for collaboration such as detailed prototyping and review cycles.

1.     Preparation Phase: Start with information gathering and a   
“Savvy Start” brainstorming session.

2.     Iterative Design Phase: Create quick prototypes (or “approximations”). Design, build a rough version, evaluate, and refine. Repeat in cycles to spot flaws fast.

3.     Iterative Development Phase: Build the prototypes into fuller versions. Develop, test, adapt, and evaluate again. Loop until the product is polished and ready for rollout.

4.     Implementation and Evaluation. Roll it our and iterate again if needed. In contrast to ADDIE evaluation is backed into every loop, not tacked onto the end like ADDIE.

## Implications

Successive Approximation Model (SAM) shifts instructional design from a rigid, linear process such as ADDIE to an agile, iterative one that prioritizes rapid prototyping, continuous feedback, and collaboration. A key implication is the heavy reliance on organizational buy-in: success demands committed resources (time, personnel, and access to SMEs, stakeholders, and potential learners) throughout the cycles of approximation. Without strong support and cross-functional participation, iterations can stall, undermining SAM's advantages in speed and quality. 

## Strengths of SAM for the Minicourse

SAM's iterative, agile nature shines in designing a compact, targeted minicourse such as a pain management modules for paramedics: rapid prototyping of case simulations for scenarios like sickle cell crisis or crush syndrome, quickly building a rough version, test it with a pilot group of novice and veteran paramedics, and refine in successive approximations. This supports the minicourse by catching usability issues early, ensuring alignment with Maryland Medical Protocols, and incorporating UDL principles, e.g., adding engagement options (videos for newbies, quick refs for vets) based on real learner input. The collaborative "Savvy Start" brainstorming can rally stakeholders to define clear goals leading to a more effective, learner-centered experience that builds decision-making confidence.

## Limitations of SAM for the Minicourse

SAM's demand for ongoing collaboration and rapid iterations can strain resources in a constrained environment, especially for a one-off course aimed at just 100 paramedics: pulling in busy partners for multiple feedback rounds might eat into limited time and personnel.
