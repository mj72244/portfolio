# Behaviorism Overview

Behaviorism is a learning theory that focuses on observable behaviors and how they can be modified by environmental stimuli. It emphasizes the role of reinforcement and punishment. It often treats the subject as a black box responding to external stimuli while ignoring intrinsic motivations.

## Ivan Pavlov

Anyone who has taken a psychology class has likely encountered Ivan Pavlov, and Pavlov's dogs have entered the popular consciousness. Pavlov started with a study to determine how much saliva different dog breeds produces for digestion. He noticed that the dogs would start to salivate before the food was provided. He realized that the dogs had associated the sound of him walking down the stairs with the arrival of food.

He tested the new theory by playing a tone when feeding the dogs. Over time the dogs learned to associate the tone with feeding, prompting the dogs to begin salivating even when the food was not present. The dogs learned a new response to the tone via stimulus association.

Pavlov's work strongly influenced the the field of behaviorism. In Condtioned  Reflexes: An Investigation of the the Physiological Activity of the Cerebral Cortex in 1927 he laid out his research. He emphasized behaviors are the product of stimulus and response. The sound of the tone caused no response until it was associated with the presentation of food which then caused the response of increased saliva response.

## John Watson

Watson is considered the founder of behaviorism. According to Watson learning is a change in observable behavior. He defined behaviorism in a 1913 article as "purely objective experimental branch of natural science". He dismissed introspective thoughts and feelings as they can not be observed and hence should be ignored when analyzing behavior.

He is remembered as the first psychologist to use human subjects. By today's standards some of his experimental work is considered unethical.

## Edward L. Thorndike

Thorndike shaped behaviorism with this Law of Effect that states that behaviors followed by a satisfying outcome are more likely to be repeated while those followed  a negative outcome will not be repeated.

In 1903 he wrote one of the first books on the subject of educational psychology, Educational Psychology.

## B.F. Skinner

Skinner expanded behaviorism by defining operant conditioning, a concept that was first studied by Thorndike. At the core of operant conditioning is the relationship between reinforcement and punishment. Rewarded behavior will more likely be repeated and punished behaviors will be less likely to be repeated. Skinner published The Behavior of Organisms in 1938 that laid out the principles of operant conditioning.

Skinner is also notable for introducing radical behaviorism which considers thoughts and emotions when analyzing behavior, broadening the scope of traditional behaviorism.

## Implications for Instructional Design

Behaviorism with its focus on observable behaviors and external stimuli has significant implications for instructional design. The theory emphasizes reinforcement, repetition, and stimulus-response associations to facilitate learning.

It informs the creation of structured learning environments where objectives are clearly defined. Content should be broken down into small manageable units with feedback that reinforces correct responses. This facilitates learners moving through the material at their own pace.

However, behaviorism's focus on measurable outcomes can limit its application to areas requiring creativity, judgment, and critical analysis which require open ended approaches.

## Strengths and Limitations in Fire/EMS Instruction

Behaviorism can excel in teaching standardized procedural tasks through repetition, practice, and reinforcement. For example it can help firefighters to master hose line advancement by practicing specific steps with instructor feedback reinforcing correct technique. EMTs and paramedics can learn patient assessments through structured drills  where correct actions are reinforced with positive feedback and successful simulation outcomes.

The need for observable outcomes also aligns with behaviorism's focus. Trainees must demonstrate specific skills: deploying ground ladders, advancing hose lines, and timely assessments of medical and trauma patients to pass certification exams.

Behaviorism is less effective for developing clinical judgment and fire ground decision making both which requires synthesizing information and making decisions in the face of uncertainty. Deciding to administer a drug in a dynamic EMS scenario, or choosing between an offensive or defensive strategy on the fire ground involves cognitive processes beyond behaviorism's stimulus-response associations.
