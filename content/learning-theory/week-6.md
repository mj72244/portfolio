# Andragogy

## Overview

Andragogy, the theory of adult learning, popularized by Malcolm Knowles in the 1970s and 1980s in book The Adult Learner: A Neglected Species, contrasts pedagogy by assuming adults are experience rich, and ready to learn for real life needs (Knowles et al., 2015) This facilitates working with the learner, emphasizing the autonomy of the learner.

It was German educator Alexander Kapp in 1833 who first introduced the term andragogy. In the 1920s Eugen Rosenstock-Huessy and Eduard Lindeman revisited the concept (eLearning Industry, 2013) but it was Knowles who elaborated it in its modern form.

In the context of EMS education, andragogy offers many strengths as most, if not all, learners are adults with prior experiences, and intrinsic motivations as EMS classes are not a required component of high school or college requirements.

A limitation however is that in demanding professions such as EMS, andragogy assumes self-direction and internal motivations which may not apply to learners experiencing burnout, or who pursued EMS credentials to land a job in a related field such as the Fire Service.

## Previous Learning Experience

As an adult learner I've experienced both formal and informal learning. I recently completed the Active Attack Integrated Response (AAIR) course through the Advanced Law Enforcement Rapid Response Training (ALERRT) program, a class focused on integrating law enforcement, fire, and EMS response in active attack scenarios. An informal experience was how to best setup a medicine infusion pump via an Instagram Reel from @the.prehospitalist.

I'll focus on the formal AAIR experience as it was memorable with its hands on simulations and real world applicability. It built confidence by building on previous educational and real world experience of handling and leading real world emergencies, but also revealed gaps in coordinating with other public safety agencies which individual first responders, and even fire department company officers, do not routinely do.

The experience was successful because it embodied key andragogy principles such as readiness to learn and relevance of content (Knowles et al., 2015). As an EMS professional I had intrinsic motivation. The class drew on my real world experience responding to high stakes calls and connecting it with low frequency, high risk scenarios using practical drills.

For incremental learning such as best practices on how to setup an infusion pump, informal learning which engages your PLN is often valuable.

For myself, for learning that builds on existing knowledge while developing new areas, formal learning is often more valuable for building new schemas for the learner.
