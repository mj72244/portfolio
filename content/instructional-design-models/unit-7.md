Learning Objectives

Course learning outcomes and learning objectives are related but distinct tools in instructional design. Learning outcomes sit at the “big picture” level for the entire course (or minicourse), while learning objectives break that picture into smaller, actionable steps that learners achieve along the way.

Course Learning Outcomes: These are high-level statements that capture the overall

knowledge, skills, or attitudes a learner should possess by the time they

finish the complete course or program. They are usually written in general terms

and provide a roadmap for educators and instructional designers.

Learning Objectives: These are specific, measurable statements that describe what a learner will know, do, or demonstrate by the end of a single lesson, module, or short activity. They act as the building blocks that lead to the broader outcomes. They are written with clear action verbs (from Bloom’s taxonomy such as analyze, understand, apply), are easier to assess directly, and help designers create targeted content, activities, and evaluations. In EMS education objectives are often written using the ABCD framework. It stands for Audience, Behavior, Condition, and Degree. An example would be:

Audience: Paramedics completing continuing education 

Behavior: select the appropriate initial analgesic agent and dosage 

Condition: given a simulated adult patient in sickle cell crisis and the Maryland Medical Protocols (2025) 

Degree: correctly (matching protocol exactly) on the first attempt

Full objective: "Given a simulated adult patient in sickle cell crisis and access to the Maryland Medical Protocols (2025), the paramedic will select the appropriate initial analgesic agent and dosage correctly on the first attempt."

Another objective:

Audience: Paramedics completing continuing education

Behavior: select the appropriate initial analgesic agent and dosage

Condition: given a simulated adult patient with a MOLST Option B form (Do Not Resuscitate) and severe pain from an obvious forearm fracture

Degree: correctly (matching protocol exactly) on the first attempt and appropriately splint the injured arm

Full objective: “Given a simulated adult patient with a MOLST Option B form, in severe pain from an obvious forearm fracture, and access to the Maryland Medical Protocols (2025), the paramedic will select the appropriate initial analgesic agent and dosage correctly on the first attempt and appropriately splint the injured arm."

These two objectives are specific and are easy to assess: did they select the correct drug and dose, and did they select the appropriate splinting technique?

Course Learning Outcomes are higher level. Two learning outcomes for my minicourse could be:

By the end of this course:

1.     Learners will be able to identify key indicators and contraindications for pain management in special-case prehospital scenarios, including sickle cell crisis, burns, renal colic (kidney stones), eye trauma, crush or compartment syndrome, dive injuries, and palliative care for patients with DNR/MOLST orders, in alignment with the Maryland Medical Protocols (MIEMSS, 2025).

2.     Apply evidence-based pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions (e.g., fentanyl, ketamine, ketorolac, positioning, and supportive care) appropriately to achieve safe and effective pain relief in uncommon scenarios while minimizing risks such as respiratory depression, hypotension, or ethical conflicts.

Bloom’s Taxonomy

A hierarchical framework that classifies educational learning objectives according to cognitive complexity. It help instructional designers, educators, and course developers create objectives and activities that progress from basic recall to critical thinking and innovation. It was developed by Benjamin Bloom in the 1950s. It was updated by cognitive psychologists and they shifted to verb-based action labels.

The taxonomy structures cognitive skills into six levels from lower levels to higher-order thinking. Each level builds on the earlier levels fostering deeper understanding and creativity. In EMS education it helps guide learning experiences from protocol recall to scenario-based decision making.

1.     Remembering Involves recalling or retrieving relevant facts, terms, and basic concepts from memory. This foundational level ensures learners have command of key facts (e.g., recalling specific analgesics listed in the Maryland Medical Protocols).

2.     Understanding Involves grasping the meaning of material by interpreting, explaining, summarizing, or demonstrating comprehension. Learners show they can make sense of information through paraphrasing, interpreting, or giving examples (e.g., explaining why certain pain management approaches differ in sickle cell crisis versus routine trauma).

3.     Applying Involves using learned information, procedures, concepts, or methods to execute tasks or solve problems. Focuses on real-world implementation (e.g., applying protocol guidelines to select and administer pain relief in a simulated burn patient scenario).

4.     Analyzing Involves breaking down information into component parts, examining relationships, patterns, causes/effects, or underlying structures, and identifying distinctions. Encourages critical examination (e.g., differentiating contraindications and relationships between patient conditions like eye trauma and compartment syndrome).

5.     Evaluating Involves making judgments, assessments, or critiques about the value, quality, effectiveness, or appropriateness of ideas, methods, or solutions based on criteria and evidence. Requires informed judgment and justification (e.g., critiquing or defending a pain management choice in a palliative care case with DNR orders for ethical and protocol compliance).

6.     Creating The highest level: involves generating new ideas, products, solutions, or ways of viewing things by reorganizing elements into novel patterns or constructing original work.

## Application of Bloom’s Taxonomy to the Paramedic Megacode

![](/images/instructional-design-models-unit-7-3.jpg)

Sara stood over the high-fidelity mannequin. The final challenge before being cleared to function independently as a paramedic in her department was to demonstrate her ability to function as a team leader during a “megacode”, demonstrating her ability to apply American Heart Association Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) and Maryland Medical Protocols to a simulated cardiac emergency in a evolving, realistic patient emergency through initial assessment through resuscitation and stabilization.

## Remembering

“Sixty-two-year-old male, sudden collapse after motor vehicle accident on the interstate. As I arrive on scene, I recall the possible underlying causes: hypoxia, hypovolemia, hydrogen ion acidosis, hypo/hyperkalemia, hypothermia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, thrombosis.”

## Understanding

Feeling for a pulse and not feeling one, Sara looked at the monitor. “This is pulseless electrical activity because the electrical system is firing but there is no mechanical output, possibly pulmonary embolism. The body is not circulating oxygen.”

## Applying

Sara directed her partner to start chest compressions at 110 a minute, at least two inches depth, and allowing full recoil. She started an IV on the mannequin and announced to the test proctor that she would push epinephrine 1mg, followed by a 10 mL flush since it was uncertain if the cause of the cardiac arrest was medical or trauma.

## Analyzing

After six minutes of CPR the end tidal CO2 (ETCo2) was below 15. Sara considered the possible underlying causes, and the effectiveness of chest compressions. She had a fresh provider switch in and directed them to compress at the appropriate depth and rate. ETCo2 increased to 18.

## Evaluating

Sara reconsidered underlying causes and that a level 1 trauma center was 7 minutes away while reviewing the cardiac arrest checklist. Lung sounds were equal bilaterally, suggesting that the underlying cause may be cardiac tamponade. She communicated the decision to start emergent transport to the trauma center for possible pericardiocentesis.

## Creating

Arrival at trauma center confirmed cardiac tamponade, and the patient was transferred to the trauma ICU after pericardiocentesis and ROSC (return of spontaneous circulation). Sara wiped her forehead. “The checklist does not cover the criteria for transport or termination of resuscitation in trauma arrest. I’ll draft some additions and run it by the medical director.”
