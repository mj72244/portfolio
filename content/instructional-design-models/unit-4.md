Understanding by Design

# Overview

Understanding by Design (UbD) is a backward planning framework for creating effective curriculum that prioritizes student understanding over simply covering content. Developed by Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe, it encourages instructional designers to start with the end in mind: what learners should truly grasp and apply long-term. Rather than beginning with activities, UbD reverses the traditional process to ensure alignment among learning goals, assessments, and instruction (Avenues The World School, 2013).

Understanding by Design consists of three stages (Reaves et al., 2019):

Stage 1: Identify Desired Results

In this stage, designers focus on what learners should know, understand, and be able to do by the end of the course or unit. When reviewing available content, it is essential to prioritize and select material thoughtfully to support clear instruction and alignment with the desired results. Key elements include identifying enduring understandings (long-term insights), essential questions to guide inquiry, and relevant knowledge and skills, while drawing from standards and curriculum expectations to establish clear priorities.

Stage 2: Determine Acceptable Evidence

Designers decide how to measure whether learners have achieved the desired results before planning instruction. This involves creating assessments such as quizzes, tests, performance tasks, and projects that provide evidence of understanding and mastery. Assessments must align with the goals identified in Stage 1.

Stage 3: Plan Learning Experiences and Instruction

After clarifying desired results and evidence of learning designers can plan instructional activities and resources. Instructional activities should directly support the goals and assessments identified in earlier stages.

# Implications for Learning Design

McTighe and Wiggins (2012) explain that by emphasizing backward planning UbD helps ensure that every element of a course aligns with meaningful outcomes. Instead of starting with content or activities designers clarify goals first leading to more purposeful learning experiences fostering transfer of knowledge. For learning designers, it means prioritizing the “big ideas” and essential questions that drive engagement, making courses more relevant. (Wiggins

 

In practice, UbD encourages the use of varied assessments and flexible instruction to accommodate diverse learners, aligning well with principles like Universal Design for Learning (UDL) from your minicourse idea. It shifts the role of instructors from "content deliverers" to "coaches of understanding," promoting active inquiry and reflection. However, it requires upfront investment in planning, which can be a shift for designers used to traditional forward-planning methods. Overall, UbD enhances curricular quality by focusing on evidence of understanding, leading to better student achievement and long-term retention.

# Evaluation of Strengths and Limitations

One key strength is the focus on backward design which ensures alignment between desired outcomes, such as building confidence in applying pain management in less common situations and learning activities such as case simulations. By identifying the big idea in stage 1: “adapting pain management protocols in uncommon scenarios optimizes patient comfort and outcomes”, leading to targeted assessments in stage 2 that support learning outcomes. This supports my minicourse by promoting transfer of knowledge to prehospital settings.

However, UbD does have limitations that challenge the design of my 45-minute microlearning course. The model can be time consuming to apply which can conflict with the requirement to create paramedic continuing education on a yearly basis. Wiggins does state that the model becomes easier to apply with time and experience, so it may be worth the upfront investment in the model (Avenues The World School, 2013).
