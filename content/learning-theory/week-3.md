# Cognitivism

## Early Contributors: Edward Tolman, Wolfgang Köhler, and Ivan Krechevsky

Early forays towards cognitivist research occurred in the 1920s and 1930s by researchers Edward Tolman, Wolfgang Köhler, and Ivan Krechevsky. Tolman (1932) was an early critic of behaviorism, demonstrating through his studies that learning was not always automatic. Tolman explored purposeful behavior in rats navigating mazes, while Köhler's work with learning in primates challenged stimulus-response models.

## Piaget

In 1952, Piaget influenced the cognitive revolution with his theory of cognitive development, which unfolds through four stages:

* Sensorimotor Stage (Birth–2 years): Infants learn through sensory experiences and motor actions and develop an understanding that objects exist even when out of sight (object permanence).
* Preoperational Stage (2–7 years): Children use symbols, learning that words and pictures can represent other things. Pretend play and imagination are essential to learning.
* Concrete Operational Stage (7–11 years): Logical thinking develops, but children still find abstract and hypothetical thinking difficult.
* Formal Operational Stage (11 years and up): Abstract thinking and hypothetical reasoning develop. Learners can reason about ideas and theoretical constructs.

## Benjamin Bloom

Benjamin Bloom chaired a committee of educators in 1956. From that committee's work emerged Bloom's taxonomy, a framework for categorizing educational goals that is still actively used by educators today. Bloom's taxonomy encourages instructional design and learning beyond rote memorization, enabling learners to apply knowledge in unfamiliar situations, analyze information, and synthesize new ideas.

## Lev Vygotsky

In the 1920s and 1930s, Vygotsky argued that cognitive development occurs through culturally mediated tools, language, and collaboration. He introduced the concepts of the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) and scaffolding.

The ZPD is the gap between what a learner can accomplish independently and what they can achieve with the guidance of a more knowledgeable other. Scaffolding is where support is tailored to the learner's needs and gradually withdrawn as competence grows.

## Jerome Bruner

In the 1960s, Bruner focused on how knowledge is represented and organized in modes of thinking or representation. He proposed three modes of representation:

* Enactive representation (action-based)
* Iconic representation (image-based)
* Symbolic representation (language-based)

Bruner emphasized that the purpose of education is not to impart knowledge but to facilitate thinking and problem-solving skills that can be applied in a range of situations. In his 1960 text, The Process of Education, the premise is that students are active learners constructing their own knowledge.

## Albert Bandura

Bandura introduced social learning theory in the 1960s and 1970s. It emphasized that learning occurs through observation, imitation, and modeling of others' behaviors.

His famous Bobo doll experiment demonstrated that children learn aggressive behaviors through observation. The group of children who observed an adult behaving aggressively toward an inflatable Bobo doll were more likely to imitate those aggressive behaviors toward the doll compared to the non-aggressive model group and the control group.

His ideas support instructional design that includes peer modeling, demonstrations, and goal-setting activities to enhance skill acquisition and behavioral change.

## Implications

The implications of cognitivism encourage instructional designers to create materials that align with cognitive processes instead of aiming for rote memorization. Instructional design should incorporate learning outcomes tied to skills like analysis and synthesis, as outlined in Bloom's taxonomy.

Integrating cognitive load theory to manage working memory limitations leads designers to sequence content to build schemas that lay the foundation for learning.

## Strengths and Limitations in Fire/EMS Education

First responders must rapidly process complex information during emergencies. Cognitivism offers notable strengths through structured mental models. Its focus on schema development helps build rapid recall, for example ABC primary assessments, by chunking and sequencing.

Metacognitive strategies, such as debriefing after simulations or calls, help with self-awareness of decision-making biases.

One limitation is its limited emphasis on stress responses. During real emergencies, adrenaline can impair working memory, yet the theory overlooks affective load (LeBlanc, 2009).

## Practical Application Example

I chose an instructional video from Khan Academy titled "Fraction word problem: pizza":<https://www.khanacademy.org/math/cc-fourth-grade-math/imp-fractions-2/imp-adding-and-subtracting-fractions-word-problems/v/fraction-word-problems-1-exercise>

The five-minute video walks the learner through fraction word problems using relatable visuals of pie and pizza slices as Sal Khan steps through solving the word problems. By making it visual, it helps engage learners as he breaks down each part of the word problem.

Khan reduces cognitive load by pasting the word problem into the drawing area so that as he works through the problem visually, the learner is not forced to either rewind the video or remember the problem. In addition, as he works through the problem, he underlines the portion under consideration. Even more, he uses the same color to underline the relevant part and to color the visual—for example, he underlines "Ishaan ate 2 slices of pizza" in red and colors in the two slices of pizza in red. Finally, he labels the pieces of pizza with a number as he counts the slices.
